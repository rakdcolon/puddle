From the editor

On running a small puzzle column.

What this is, what to expect, and who's writing it down.

Puddle started as a notebook. Most mornings I'd find a puzzle in an interview-prep book, on a problem set, or buried in a textbook footnote — write it down, scribble until I solved it, and then forget about it by lunch. This column is that notebook, posted publicly, one puzzle a day.

The aim is small: a quiet five to ten minutes in the morning, somewhere between the kettle and the first email. One problem at a time. No streaks, no leaderboards, no notifications nagging you to come back. The puzzles are meant to be chewed on, not collected.

· · ·

On the puzzles

Each day's puzzle is drawn from one of six genres — logic, deduction, quant, sequences, lateral riddles, and wordplay. The sources are honest: classic brain teasers, quant-interview questions, competition math problems, and the occasional original. Difficulty ranges from gentle (a five-minute warm-up) to wicked (a problem you may end up thinking about all week).

Every puzzle ships with a graduated hint trail. You can solve it cold, you can take a single nudge, or you can walk through three progressively heavier hints until the answer is in sight. Hints cost nothing — the idea is to remove guilt from asking.

· · ·

On the design

The visual language is deliberately quiet. Cream paper, walnut ink, one rust accent, and a single serif. There are no avatars, no badges, no progress bars badgering you for engagement. The page should feel closer to a newspaper puzzle column than a mobile game — something to read, not something to tap through.

The product is free, and it's likely to stay that way. If you want to support the column, the best thing you can do is submit a puzzle you've enjoyed.
